# Positioning Playbook

Reference guide for competitive positioning, differentiation, and messaging strategy. Load on demand during Positioning and Landscape modes.

---

## 1. Positioning Canvas

Fill in all five elements. If any is vague, the positioning is incomplete.

| Element | Question | Bad Example | Good Example |
|---------|----------|-------------|--------------|
| **Audience** | Who exactly? | "Small businesses" | "Freelance designers with 1-5 clients" |
| **Problem** | What specific pain? | "Project management is hard" | "Losing track of revision requests across email, Slack, and comments" |
| **Solution** | What do you do? | "AI-powered platform" | "Single inbox that collects all client feedback across channels" |
| **Differentiation** | Why you, not them? | "Better UX" | "Only tool that auto-extracts action items from Slack threads" |
| **Proof** | Why believe you? | "Trusted by thousands" | "Used by 200 freelancers, 4.8 avg rating on Product Hunt" |

**The positioning statement**:
> For [audience] who [problem], [product name] is the [category] that [differentiation]. Unlike [primary competitor], we [key difference].

## 2. Seven Differentiation Axes

Choose ONE primary axis. Trying to differentiate on everything = differentiating on nothing.

### Price
**Strategy**: Serve the tier below your competitors' cheapest plan.
**When it works**: Mature market, established features, competitors over-charge.
**Solo founder fit**: Good — lower overhead means you can sustain lower prices.
**Risk**: Race to bottom, attracts price-sensitive churners.
**Example**: Plausible vs Google Analytics — simpler, cheaper, privacy-focused.

### Niche
**Strategy**: Serve one specific audience better than anyone.
**When it works**: Broad competitors neglect specific segments.
**Solo founder fit**: Excellent — this is the #1 strategy for solo founders.
**Risk**: Market too small, hard to expand later.
**Example**: Descript for podcasters vs Adobe Premiere for everyone.

### Experience
**Strategy**: Make the complex thing dead simple.
**When it works**: Existing tools are powerful but have steep learning curves.
**Solo founder fit**: Good — solo founders can obsess over UX.
**Risk**: Feature-poor perception, hard to match functionality.
**Example**: Linear vs Jira — same problem, radically simpler experience.

### Tech
**Strategy**: Proprietary technology that enables something competitors can't do.
**When it works**: You have deep domain expertise or a novel technical approach.
**Solo founder fit**: Possible if you have a technical edge.
**Risk**: Technology alone isn't a moat without distribution.
**Example**: Superhuman's speed optimizations vs Gmail.

### Integration
**Strategy**: Fit perfectly into existing workflows rather than replacing them.
**When it works**: Users are locked into an ecosystem and want enhancements, not replacements.
**Solo founder fit**: Excellent — build on top of platforms.
**Risk**: Platform dependency, API changes can kill you.
**Example**: Loom integrating into Slack/Notion vs standalone video platform.

### Speed
**Strategy**: Be the fastest way to accomplish the task.
**When it works**: Users value time over features.
**Solo founder fit**: Good — small products can be faster than bloated ones.
**Risk**: Speed is easy to replicate.
**Example**: Vercel's one-click deploy vs manual AWS setup.

### Simplicity
**Strategy**: Remove features instead of adding them. Do less, better.
**When it works**: Market is full of bloated products. Users only need 20% of features.
**Solo founder fit**: Excellent — less to build, less to maintain.
**Risk**: "Too simple" perception, hard to expand.
**Example**: Bear vs Notion — just notes, nothing else.

## 3. Wedge Strategy Patterns

How to enter a market as a solo founder. Choose one.

### Pattern 1: Niche Down
**How**: Pick the most underserved segment. Own it completely. Expand later.
**Signal it's right**: Broad competitors have forums full of complaints from a specific user type.
**First 100 users**: Go where the niche hangs out (subreddit, Discord, Slack group). Solve their #1 pain.
**Example**: ConvertKit niched into "email for bloggers" when Mailchimp served everyone.

### Pattern 2: Platform Wedge
**How**: Build the best tool on top of an existing platform's ecosystem.
**Signal it's right**: Popular platform with an app store/marketplace and gaps.
**First 100 users**: Launch in the platform's marketplace. Built-in distribution.
**Example**: Calendly started as a simple scheduling link before becoming a platform.

### Pattern 3: Workflow Integration
**How**: Don't replace anything. Fit between existing tools.
**Signal it's right**: Users duct-tape multiple tools together with Zapier/manual copy-paste.
**First 100 users**: Show the "before (3 tools, 10 clicks)" vs "after (1 click)" demo.
**Example**: Zapier itself — doesn't replace any tool, connects all of them.

### Pattern 4: Pricing Disrupt
**How**: Offer 80% of the leader's features at 20% of the price.
**Signal it's right**: Dominant player has enterprise pricing but a large underserved SMB/prosumer tier.
**First 100 users**: Target users who are searching for "[dominant player] alternatives" or "[dominant player] too expensive."
**Example**: Plausible vs Google Analytics (free but privacy-invasive) — simple + paid + private.

### Pattern 5: Experience Simplify
**How**: Take a complex product category and make it ridiculously simple.
**Signal it's right**: Existing tools require training, documentation, onboarding.
**First 100 users**: Tweet/post the "I just did [complex thing] in 30 seconds" demo. Virality through simplicity.
**Example**: Carrd vs WordPress — one-page sites in minutes, not hours.

## 4. Battles to Avoid

Hard rules for solo founders. Do NOT fight these battles head-on.

### Funding Gap > 10x
If a direct competitor has raised 10x+ your available capital, do NOT compete on:
- Marketing spend
- Sales team size
- Feature breadth
- Enterprise deals

**Instead**: Go where they can't — niche communities, speed of iteration, personal relationship with users.

### Network Effects Already Established
If a competitor's product gets better as more people use it AND they already have critical mass, do NOT try to:
- Build the same network from scratch
- Offer "the same but better"

**Instead**: Find a use case that doesn't require the network, or build on top of their network.

### Platform Owner is Competing
If the platform you build on launches a competing feature:
- Do NOT assume you can out-feature the platform
- Do NOT panic and pivot immediately

**Instead**: Go deeper into your niche. Platforms build for 80% use case; you own the 20% edge cases.

### Brand Trust in Regulated Spaces
If competitors have established trust in compliance-heavy spaces (finance, health, legal):
- Do NOT try to enter regulated spaces without credentials

**Instead**: Serve the adjacent non-regulated use case. Or partner with a credentialed player.

## 5. Comparison Handling Templates

For every direct competitor, prepare a response for "How are you different from [X]?"

### Template A: When you're simpler
> "[X] is a powerful tool for [their use case]. We focus specifically on [your niche] — so instead of configuring [complex thing], you just [simple action]. If you need [enterprise feature], [X] is probably better. If you want [specific outcome] in [time], that's what we're built for."

### Template B: When you're cheaper
> "[X] is great for [their audience]. Our pricing is designed for [your audience] — you get [core features] for [price], without paying for [enterprise features] you don't need."

### Template C: When you're more focused
> "We overlap with [X] on [shared feature], but we go much deeper on [your specialty]. If [competitor use case] is your main need, [X] works well. If [your use case] is your priority, we're purpose-built for that."

### Template D: When they're bigger
> "[X] is an established player with [their strength]. We're [smaller/newer], which means [advantage: faster iteration, personal support, opinionated design]. We're best for [specific user type] who values [your differentiation]."

## 6. Messaging Framework

Once positioning is clear, structure messaging in this order:

### Headline
**Formula**: [Outcome] for [Audience] — without [Pain]
**Example**: "Client feedback organized in seconds — without chasing emails"

### Subheadline
**Formula**: [Product] is a [category] that [key action]. Built for [audience].
**Example**: "FeedbackBox is a client communication hub that collects revisions from everywhere. Built for freelance designers."

### Three Proof Points
Pick 3 specific capabilities that reinforce differentiation:
1. Feature that competitor lacks
2. Experience advantage (speed, simplicity)
3. Social proof or outcome metric

### CTA
Aligned with wedge strategy:
- Niche down → "Join 200 freelance designers using FeedbackBox"
- Pricing disrupt → "Get started free — no credit card"
- Experience simplify → "Try it now — takes 30 seconds"

## 7. Positioning Anti-Patterns

Do NOT do these:

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails | Instead |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| "Cheaper version of [X]" | Positions you as inferior by default | Find your own positioning axis |
| "Like [X] but with AI" | AI is a feature, not positioning | Position around the outcome AI enables |
| "All-in-one platform" | Can't out-feature funded teams | Own one thing completely |
| "For everyone" | Speaks to no one specifically | Pick a niche, even if it feels small |
| "Better UX" | Subjective and unverifiable | Show specific workflow improvement with time savings |
| Competing on features list | Always lose to bigger teams | Compete on focus, speed, or experience |
| Negative positioning ("unlike [X], we don't...") | Makes you seem reactive and small | Positive positioning ("we're built for [specific need]") |
